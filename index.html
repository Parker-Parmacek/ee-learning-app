ARC FLASH / ENERGY PATH

Why clearing time changes everything.

Follow an arc-flash study from the one-line diagram to the equipment label: establish the system, predict the arc, interrogate the protective device, calculate exposure, and choose risk controls.

LearnTrace every stage of an IEEE 1584 study.
ExploreConnect device curves, time, distance, and energy.
CheckTest ten study and field concepts.

A chain, not a single number

Fault current matters, but the protection system decides how long the arc is fed. Working distance then sets the exposure at the worker.

01 — ESTABLISH

Start with the system

Build the one-line, validate utility and transformer data, define operating modes, and calculate available bolted fault current at each bus. Then document voltage, enclosure dimensions, conductor gap, grounding, and electrode configuration.

02 — INTERROGATE

Predict the arc, then read the TCC

Convert bolted fault current into arcing current with the IEEE 1584 model. Plot that arcing current—and the required reduced-current case—against the exact upstream protective-device curve and settings to obtain clearing time.

03 — EVALUATE

Calculate exposure at the worker

Calculate incident energy at the task-specific working distance, determine the distance at which energy falls to 1.2 cal/cm², document assumptions, and use the result inside the electrical-safety risk assessment.

Ibf

Bolted fault current

Prospective current with negligible fault impedance. It is an input to the arc-current model.

Iarc

Arcing current

Current through the plasma arc. It can place the protective device on a slower part of its curve.

E

Incident energy

Predicted thermal energy at a stated working distance, commonly reported in cal/cm².

The counterintuitive part

Lower arcing current does not always mean lower incident energy. If the lower current misses an instantaneous trip and lands in a delayed region, the longer duration can dominate.

01 / PHYSICS

What actually happens during an arc flash

An arc flash is not just a short circuit with a bright spark. It is a self-sustaining electrical discharge through ionized air that converts electrical energy into intense heat, light, pressure, sound, and molten material.

  1. Initiation

    Insulation failure, contamination, a dropped tool, loose connection, animal contact, or a worker’s probe can create an initial conductive path between energized conductors or from phase to ground.

  2. Ionization

    The initial current heats and ionizes the air. The air changes from an insulator into conductive plasma, allowing current to continue flowing even if the initiating object moves away.

  3. Arc growth

    The plasma channel expands and can bridge phases. Metal at the electrodes vaporizes, adding conductive material and releasing hot gases and droplets. In an enclosure, the geometry directs much of that energy out through the opening.

  4. Energy release

    The upstream source continues feeding the arc. The event persists until a protective device opens, the arc becomes unstable and extinguishes, or a conductor separates. For most studies, protective-device clearing time is the variable engineers can most directly influence.

Do not merge the hazards: arc flash addresses thermal exposure. Arc blast pressure, sound, flying debris, electric shock, and secondary falls are separate hazards that need their own controls.

02 / INPUTS

Build the model before calculating energy

A credible result depends on a credible power-system model. The calculation is downstream of field data, one-line accuracy, equipment condition, and protective-device settings.

SOURCE

Available short-circuit current

Use utility contribution, generators, transformers, motors, cable impedance, and each realistic operating configuration. Maximum and minimum source cases can produce different outcomes.

EQUIPMENT

Voltage and enclosure

Record nominal voltage, enclosure height, width and depth, conductor gap, equipment class, and whether the electrodes are in open air or inside a box.

GEOMETRY

Electrode configuration

IEEE 1584-2018 distinguishes configurations such as vertical conductors in a box, vertical conductors terminating in a barrier, and horizontal conductors. Orientation changes how energy is directed.

PROTECTION

Device and settings

Use the exact fuse or breaker curve, sensor and plug rating, long-time, short-time, instantaneous, and ground-fault settings, plus any maintenance-mode or zone-selective-interlocking behavior that applies.

TASK

Working distance

Measure from the likely arc source to the worker’s face and torso for the task. A label value calculated at one distance cannot be silently reused at a closer distance.

VALIDATION

Field verification

Confirm equipment labels, conductor lengths, transformer impedance, breaker settings, and normal operating lineup. A polished report cannot rescue stale or incorrect inputs.

03 / CURRENT

Bolted fault current is not arcing current

The short-circuit study establishes available bolted fault current. IEEE 1584 then predicts the smaller current that flows through the arc. That distinction controls which part of the protective-device curve is used.

Ibf → IEEE 1584 arc model → Iarc → protective-device TCC → t

Do not take clearing time at Ibf and carry it into the energy calculation. Plot Iarc on the actual device curve. IEEE 1584-2018 also requires an arcing-current variation calculation; the reduced-current case can clear more slowly and can govern the final result.

  1. Calculate Ibf at the equipment bus

    This is the prospective current for a zero-impedance connection. It describes source strength, not the current through a plasma arc.

  2. Predict Iarc for the equipment geometry

    Voltage, gap, enclosure size, and electrode orientation all affect the result. Arcing current is usually below bolted fault current.

  3. Evaluate normal and reduced Iarc

    Plot both on the device curve. If one value falls below instantaneous pickup, its longer clearing time may create the higher incident energy.

  4. Respect the model’s scope

    IEEE 1584-2018 covers modeled three-phase AC equipment from 208 V through 15 kV at 50 or 60 Hz and within specified parameter ranges. It is not a universal model for single-phase systems, DC systems, every equipment type, or every geometry.

04 / TIME

Read clearing time without shortcuts

The TCC is the bridge between arcing current and event duration. A small horizontal move on the curve can cause a very large vertical move in time.

  1. Identify the device that actually clears the arc

    Follow the one-line upstream from the fault location. Check whether a main, feeder, fuse, relay, differential scheme, or other device operates first for that location and lineup.

  2. Plot arcing current through the full tolerance band

    A breaker or fuse curve is a band, not an infinitely thin line. Study assumptions should define whether total clearing time, maximum tolerance, and relay plus breaker opening time are included.

  3. Check each operating mode

    Tie positions, generators, alternate feeds, maintenance switches, and zone-selective interlocking can change both fault current and clearing time. The highest fault current is not automatically the worst arc-flash case.

  4. Apply a documented time cap carefully

    Where engineering judgment supports a maximum duration, document the basis and verify egress and worker interaction assumptions. A time cap is not permission to ignore a slow or non-operating protective device.

05 / ENERGY

Translate duration and distance into exposure

Incident energy is thermal energy arriving on a surface at a specified distance, reported in cal/cm². It is not the total energy released by the arc and it is not a probability score.

Working distance

The calculation point from the prospective arc source to the worker’s face and torso during a specific task. This is an input to the incident-energy result.

Arc-flash boundary

The distance where predicted incident energy is 1.2 cal/cm². A person crossing it needs the controls and PPE required by the electrical-safety program.

Shock approach boundaries

Limited and restricted approach boundaries address electric shock, not thermal energy. They are determined separately and must not be substituted for the arc-flash boundary.

06 / FIELD USE

Turn the study into a safe work decision

The calculation describes severity at a location. NFPA 70E’s risk-assessment process asks whether a hazard exists, estimates likelihood and severity, and applies controls. A label is an input—not the entire assessment.

01

Eliminate

Establish an electrically safe work condition whenever possible.

02

Substitute

Use lower-energy methods or equipment when the design permits.

03

Engineer

Reduce clearing time, add differential protection, remote operation, arc-resistant gear, or current-limiting devices.

04

Administer

Plan the task, define approach, barricade, train, maintain equipment, and follow energized-work procedures.

05

Protect

Select arc-rated clothing and PPE with a rating at least equal to the assessed incident energy when the incident-energy method is used.

  1. Read the label in context

    Confirm equipment identity, nominal voltage, arc-flash boundary, incident energy and working distance—or the PPE category information used by the site—and the study date or document reference required by the facility program.

  2. Verify the task and equipment condition

    Normal operation of properly installed and maintained equipment with doors closed is different from opening covers, racking, testing, troubleshooting, or working on equipment with evidence of impending failure.

  3. Do not mix PPE methods

    NFPA 70E permits either the incident-energy analysis method or the task-table/PPE-category method when its conditions are satisfied. Do not calculate incident energy and then assign a PPE category from the tables as though the two scales were interchangeable.

  4. Keep the study current

    Review when system changes could affect results and on the periodic schedule required by the electrical-safety program. Utility changes, transformer replacement, conductor changes, and revised trip settings are common triggers.

WHAT THE CONTROLS TEACH

Longer and heavier means more drop

Voltage drop rises in direct proportion to one-way distance and load current. Increasing conductor circular-mil area pushes it down. System voltage does not change drop volts in this simplified current-based model, but a higher source voltage makes the same drop a smaller percentage.

NEC RECOMMENDATION

Use 3% and 5% as design checks

NEC informational notes commonly recommend limiting voltage drop to about 3% on a feeder or branch circuit and about 5% total across feeder plus branch circuit to the farthest outlet.

These are general design recommendations, not a substitute for mandatory limits that apply to certain equipment or installations.

NEC ARTICLE 220 / LOADS

Four numbers, four meanings

A defensible load calculation keeps the connected total, permitted demand reductions, continuous-load treatment, and final sizing basis separate.

01 / CONNECTED

Start with nameplate VA

Count the load that could be connected: lighting, receptacles, equipment, appliances, motors, and HVAC. Convert everything to a common unit before combining it.

02 / DEMAND

Apply only allowed factors

A demand factor recognizes that some groups are unlikely to operate at full connected load together. It is permission from the applicable Code method—not a general safety margin.

03 / DURATION

Identify continuous load

A continuous load is expected to run at maximum current for three hours or more. Feeder and branch-circuit sizing rules commonly require 125% of continuous load plus 100% of noncontinuous load.

Connected VA × demand factor = demand VA
Calculated VA = noncontinuous demand VA + 1.25 × continuous demand VA This sequence is a learning model. Specific occupancies and load types can require a different order of operations, special tables, largest-motor treatment, neutral calculations, or simultaneous-load rules.

01 / DEMAND

Demand factor is not an engineering guess

Article 220 contains different methods and tables for different occupancies and load classes. A factor that is valid for one group of loads may be prohibited for another.

CONNECTED LOAD

The arithmetic starting point

Sum the applicable nameplate or unit loads before any permitted reduction. This preserves an auditable trail from plans and schedules to the final calculation.

DEMAND LOAD

The Code-adjusted portion

Multiply a qualifying load or load block by the demand factor assigned by the chosen calculation method. Keep each block separate until its rule is applied.

DIVERSITY

Related, but not interchangeable

Diversity describes noncoincident peaks among individual loads. NEC demand factors encode specific allowances; a designer cannot substitute an unsupported diversity assumption.

METHOD

Standard or optional

Use one permitted method consistently for the occupancy and verify every eligibility condition. Do not mix favorable pieces from separate methods.

02 / DURATION

The three-hour definition determines whether the continuous-load multiplier applies. It does not mean a load receives a 75% demand factor.

Reason through the load

  1. Continuous portion: 12,000 × 1.25 = 15,000 VA.
  2. Noncontinuous portion: 18,000 × 1.00 = 18,000 VA.
  3. Calculated load: 15,000 + 18,000 = 33,000 VA.
  4. Balanced three-phase current: 33,000 ÷ (√3 × 208) ≈ 91.6 A.

Keep the distinction: the 125% continuous-load treatment supports conductor and overcurrent-device sizing. Demand factors reduce qualifying connected load under the applicable load-calculation method. They answer different questions.

03 / REVIEW

What a panel schedule cannot decide for you

A spreadsheet can add numbers, but the engineer still has to identify the governing article, system arrangement, and loads that can actually operate together.

  1. Confirm the adopted edition and local amendments

    Section numbers, table locations, and permitted methods can change by edition and jurisdiction. Cite the rule used beside each reduction.

  2. Separate service, feeder, and branch-circuit questions

    The calculation used to estimate a service is not automatically the same check used to size every downstream branch circuit.

  3. Check special loads independently

    Motors, HVAC, electric vehicle supply equipment, kitchens, dryers, ranges, energy-management systems, and nonlinear loads can introduce additional rules beyond this simplified workflow.

  4. Reconcile with the one-line and panel schedule

    Verify phase allocation, voltage, spare capacity, equipment ratings, neutral treatment, and the load totals carried upstream.
